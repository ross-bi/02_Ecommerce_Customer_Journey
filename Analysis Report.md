# E‑Commerce Customer Journey Analysis Report
## Executive Summary
This report analyses a GA4-based e‑commerce dataset for November 2020 to understand how users move through the purchase funnel, how conversion performance varies by traffic source and device, and which customer segments contribute the most revenue and lifetime value. The underlying pipeline extracts Google Analytics 4 events from BigQuery into PostgreSQL, models a star schema using dbt, and surfaces insights via a 3‑page Power BI dashboard focused on funnel performance, traffic efficiency, and customer value.

Overall funnel performance shows that roughly 1 in 4 sessions reach a product view, but only about 1.6 percent of sessions result in a purchase, generating around 144 thousand in total revenue during the month. Conversion rates and revenue contribution vary significantly by traffic source, medium, device category, and country, highlighting clear optimisation levers around organic search, referral traffic, and high-LTV geographies.
## Data & Architecture Overview
The analysis uses the GA4 Obfuscated Sample E‑Commerce dataset published as a BigQuery public dataset, restricted to the 30 days of November 2020. After filtering to core funnel events (`session_start`, `view_item`, `add_to_cart`, `begin_checkout`, `purchase`), the pipeline processes approximately 274 thousand events, 104 thousand sessions, and 79 thousand users, with total recorded revenue of about 144 thousand.

Raw events are exported from BigQuery via SQL into CSV and loaded into a PostgreSQL schema `raw.ga4_events` using scripted `CREATE TABLE` and `COPY` commands. A dbt project then builds a two-layer transformation: an incremental staging model `stg_ga4_events` that cleans and normalises GA4 fields, and a marts layer that materialises `fact_sessions`, `dim_customers`, and `dim_traffic` as a star schema for analytics.
### Star Schema Design
The marts layer centres on `fact_sessions`, which holds one row per session with binary flags for each funnel stage and the total revenue per session. `dim_customers` summarises each user’s main country, lifetime purchase revenue, and total order count, while `dim_traffic` provides a surrogate-keyed dimension over traffic source and medium, enabling efficient joins from the fact table.

The entity–relationship design enforces a many-to-one relationship from sessions to customers and from sessions to traffic, supporting consistent drill-downs from high-level KPIs to customer and channel segments. All mart models are covered by 21 schema tests (unique, not_null, accepted_values, and relationships), and combined with staging tests there are 26 tests in total, all passing in the current run.
## Data Transformation & Quality Controls
### Staging Transformations
`stg_ga4_events` is an incremental model keyed by session, which standardises device, geography, traffic, and revenue fields before they reach the marts layer. Placeholder strings such as `(not set)`, `(data deleted)`, `<Other>`, and `(none)` are mapped to more meaningful categories like `Unknown` or `Direct` for traffic, and missing revenue values are filled with zero to avoid undercounting purchases with null amounts.

Incremental processing filters to events newer than the current maximum `event_time`, providing better performance on large event logs at the cost of requiring occasional full refreshes when transformation logic changes. Traffic surrogate keys are generated using `dbt_utils.generate_surrogate_key` over `traffic_source` and `traffic_medium`, ensuring stable joins between sessions and the traffic dimension even when string values change casing or include nulls.
### Data Quality Testing
The project uses dbt’s built-in schema tests to enforce primary-key uniqueness and non-null constraints across the fact and dimension tables, including `session_id`, `customer_id`, and `traffic_sk`. Accepted-values tests ensure, for example, that device categories fall within an expected set and that funnel flags are limited to 0 or 1, protecting downstream calculations from unexpected encodings.

Relationship tests validate referential integrity between `fact_sessions.customer_id` and `dim_customers`, as well as between `fact_sessions.traffic_sk` and `dim_traffic`, ensuring that all sessions roll up cleanly to known customers and traffic rows. A full `dbt test` run passes all 26 tests with zero warnings or errors, providing strong confidence in metric correctness for the reporting layer.
## Purchase Funnel Performance
### Overall Funnel Metrics
Pre-aggregated results from the `SQL2` analysis query summarise the purchase funnel across all sessions. During the month, there are 104,264 sessions, of which 103,648 register a `session_start`, 25,717 include at least one product view, 2,178 add items to cart, 4,575 begin checkout, and 1,616 result in a purchase.

The view-rate is about 24.7 percent of sessions, meaning roughly one in four sessions reaches a product view stage. The overall conversion rate from session to purchase is 1.55 percent, while the intermediate step conversion rates are 8.47 percent from view to add-to-cart, 210.06 percent from add-to-cart to begin-checkout, and 35.32 percent from begin-checkout to purchase, reflecting the anomaly where `begin_checkout` counts exceed `add_to_cart` counts.

Total recorded revenue for the period is approximately 144,260, implying an average revenue per session of about 1.38 and a revenue-per-purchasing-session (average order value at the funnel level) close to 89. These metrics establish a baseline funnel against which channel, device, and geography breakdowns can be evaluated.
### Interpreting the Add-to-Cart vs Begin-Checkout Anomaly
The fact that the count of sessions with `begin_checkout` (4,575) is more than double those with `add_to_cart` (2,178) is consistent with prior investigation of GA4 behaviour. Many sessions trigger checkout without a corresponding `add_to_cart` event, likely due to flows where customers resume already-populated carts, use "Buy Now" actions, or where certain implementations omit the add-to-cart event on specific paths.

For analytical purposes, this means the `add_to_cart` stage cannot be treated as a strict prerequisite for checkout in this dataset. When presenting the funnel, it is important to emphasise that the most reliable measures of progression are from session start to view, and from session to purchase, while intermediate counts reflect implementation quirks as well as user behaviour.
## Traffic & Device Performance
### Conversion and Revenue by Source, Medium, and Device
The `SQL3` analysis summarises funnel performance by traffic source, medium, and device category, highlighting which combinations drive both volume and efficiency. Organic Google traffic on desktop records 18,182 sessions, with 4,352 product views, 358 add-to-cart events, 714 checkouts, and 203 purchases, corresponding to a 1.12 percent session-to-purchase conversion rate and 16,431 in revenue.

Desktop direct traffic tagged as `Direct / Unknown` contributes 13,921 sessions, 196 purchases, and 17,597 in revenue at a higher 1.41 percent conversion rate, indicating that returning or loyal users who access the site directly convert more efficiently than average. Mobile organic Google traffic produces 12,804 sessions and 186 purchases with a 1.45 percent conversion rate and 19,649 in revenue, making it one of the strongest performers among mobile traffic sources in both scale and efficiency.
### Role of Referral Traffic
Referral traffic from `shop.googlemerchandisestore.com` on desktop generates 5,130 sessions, 114 purchases, and about 10,599 in revenue, with a 2.22 percent conversion rate that significantly exceeds overall funnel performance. On mobile, the same referral source drives 3,684 sessions, 85 purchases, and 6,986 in revenue at a 2.31 percent conversion rate, confirming the strength of this partner or internal cross-linking path.

Other referral sources grouped under `Unknown / referral` on desktop and mobile show conversion rates around 1.66–1.88 percent and revenue between roughly 5,498 and 9,702, still above the global conversion benchmark. Collectively, this suggests that referral traffic, particularly from the official merchandise store, is a high-value acquisition channel that merits ongoing investment or optimisation of link placement.
### Device-Level Observations
Across sources, desktop sessions generally show strong revenue contributions, with multiple desktop segments yielding conversion rates in the 1.4–2.2 percent range. Mobile devices, while slightly less efficient in some segments, still demonstrate competitive conversion for organic and referral traffic, and often deliver substantial revenue volumes given their higher traffic share.

Tablet traffic is smaller in scale but includes some high-performing niches; for example, referral tablet traffic from `shop.googlemerchandisestore.com` reaches a 3.13 percent conversion rate with 857 in revenue from only 224 sessions. Paid search (`google / cpc`) across devices has relatively modest conversion (around 1.2–1.3 percent) and limited revenue, indicating that in this sample, paid campaigns underperform compared with organic and referral channels.
## Customer Value & Geographic Segmentation
### Country-Level Lifetime Value and Behaviour
The `SQL4_ver.A` analysis aggregates customer behaviour and lifetime value by main country, combining funnel metrics with LTV and order counts. The United States generates 45,784 sessions with an average lifetime value of 4.20, average total orders of 0.06 per customer, and 712 purchases, contributing about 64,443 in revenue and an average order value around 90.51.

India and Canada each contribute around 9,862 and 7,691 sessions respectively, with average LTVs above 4.1 and conversion rates close to 1.56–1.76 percent, and revenues of 14,304 and 12,591. Several mid-sized markets such as Spain, Turkey, and Brazil exhibit higher average LTVs in the 5–9 range and conversion rates around 1.5–1.8 percent, with average order values often exceeding 90, indicating a mix of moderate volume and high-quality purchasers.
### High-LTV Niches and Long-Tail Countries
Smaller markets like Finland, Nigeria, Greece, Vietnam, Kuwait, and Bahrain show exceptionally high average LTVs ranging from roughly 9.5 up to more than 25, albeit on relatively small customer and session bases. For example, Finland records only 165 sessions but an average LTV of 22.70, three purchases, and 561 in revenue with an average order value of 187, while Bahrain shows an average LTV of 25.75 with a single purchase yielding 206 in revenue.

These high-LTV niches represent potential premium segments where targeted campaigns or localisation could yield outsized returns relative to traffic volume. Conversely, certain countries produce substantial session volume but low or zero recorded purchases and LTV—for instance, Norway has 173 sessions and multiple product interactions but no purchases in the period, suggesting friction, price sensitivity, or localisation gaps.
### Revenue Concentration and Risk
Revenue is concentrated in a set of core markets (United States, India, Canada, the United Kingdom, Spain, Turkey, and a cluster of other large economies), which together account for a significant majority of the 144 thousand total revenue. This concentration creates both opportunity and risk: optimising experiences for these top markets can quickly improve overall performance, but over-reliance on a few geographies may increase vulnerability to regional demand shocks or policy changes.

At the same time, there is a long tail of countries with modest traffic and one or two purchases, where a small number of high-value orders drive most of the local revenue. For these markets, lightweight localisation, shipping options, and payment method support may unlock additional demand without the need for large-scale marketing investment.
## Customer-Level Behaviour Insights
### Linking Sessions to Lifetime Value
`dim_customers` provides per-customer metrics for lifetime revenue and total orders, enabling analysts to attribute session behaviour and funnel progression to underlying customer value. Customers with higher `total_orders` and `lifetime_value` appear disproportionately in the purchasing stages of the funnel and within high-performing traffic segments such as organic search and referrals, as reflected in the aggregated country and channel metrics.

Because the mart schema ties `fact_sessions.customer_id` to `dim_customers.customer_id`, it is straightforward to compute per-customer KPIs such as session frequency, purchase frequency, and average order value, and to rank customers or segments by contribution to total revenue. The precomputed `SQL5` output further supports this by summarising customer-level behaviour, allowing identification of top customers and clusters of repeat purchasers, though those detailed rows are not reproduced in full here.
### Segmenting by Lifetime Value
While the database-level `dim_customers` table stores raw `lifetime_value` and `total_orders`, the Power BI dashboard layers on calculated LTV segments to group customers into bands such as VIP, High, Mid, and Low. This segmentation is visually represented on the third dashboard page via LTV distributions, scatter plots of LTV versus total orders, and top-customer tables, providing an intuitive view of which segments generate most revenue and how frequently they purchase.

These LTV segments can be cross-filtered against traffic source, device, and geography, enabling questions such as which channels are better at acquiring high-LTV customers, and whether certain markets are over-indexed on low-value or one-time purchasers. In practice, this supports strategic decisions on budget allocation, retention programmes, and localisation priorities.
## Dashboard Design & Storytelling
### Page 1 – Executive Overview
The first Power BI page presents high-level KPIs including total revenue, total sessions, overall conversion rate, and average order value, alongside a purchase funnel visual, device breakdowns, and revenue by country. This layout allows business stakeholders to quickly assess top-line performance and spot anomalies, such as the imbalance between checkout and add-to-cart counts or unusually strong performance in particular countries.

Supporting visuals such as maps and bar charts by country highlight geographic revenue distribution, while device and traffic summaries reveal whether mobile, desktop, or tablet experiences are underperforming or excelling. Filters and slicers allow interactive focus on specific periods, devices, or channels, though the current dataset covers only a single month.
### Page 2 – Conversion & Traffic Detail
The second page dives deeper into conversion metrics by traffic source and medium, often using matrix visuals to display conversion rate, sessions, and revenue across combinations of source, medium, and device. This view makes it easy to compare, for example, organic versus paid search on mobile and desktop, or referral performance from specific domains.

KPIs such as sessions, purchases, conversion rate, and revenue are displayed side by side, enabling quick identification of channels that are both high-volume and high-efficiency—for instance, organic Google traffic on mobile and desktop, and referral traffic from the merchandise store. Poorly performing combinations, like certain paid search segments with low revenue and average conversion, are also immediately visible, guiding optimisation or budget reallocation.
### Page 3 – Customer Segments
The third page focuses on customer segmentation and LTV, including visuals of lifetime value distribution, LTV versus total orders, and ranked lists of top customers by revenue. Country and traffic-based slicers connect this page back to acquisition and behaviour insights, allowing analysts to see whether high-LTV customers cluster in particular geographies or come predominantly from specific acquisition channels.

This design supports a full narrative from acquisition to long-term value: Page 2 explains which channels bring traffic and conversions today, while Page 3 explains which of those conversions actually translate into high-LTV customers and repeat orders over time. Together, the dashboard pages deliver a coherent, business-friendly view of the customer journey.
## Key Business Insights
### Funnel Health and Immediate Opportunities
The overall funnel shows that many users reach the site (over 104 thousand sessions) but only a quarter view products and fewer than 2 percent complete purchases, leaving significant room for optimising product discovery, cart, and checkout experiences. Given the implementation quirk where many checkouts occur without add-to-cart events, focusing measurement on view-to-purchase and session-to-purchase conversion rates may provide more reliable baselines for A/B testing.

Improving product listing relevance, on-site search, and recommendation experiences could increase the proportion of sessions with product views, while simplifying checkout flows and addressing potential friction points (such as payment methods or form complexity) may boost the conversion from checkout to purchase. Even small gains in these stages would have a leveraged impact on total revenue given the large session base.
### Channel Mix and Marketing Efficiency
Organic search (particularly Google) and referral traffic consistently deliver healthy conversion rates and substantial revenue, often outperforming paid search on both metrics. This suggests that investment in search engine optimisation, content, and partnerships that generate high-quality referrals may yield better returns than incremental spending on the current paid search campaigns.

Direct traffic also performs strongly, especially on desktop, indicating a base of loyal or repeat visitors who are already familiar with the brand and convert efficiently. Retention programmes, personalised offers, or email remarketing to this base could further increase their LTV and order frequency at relatively low acquisition cost.
### Geographic Strategy and Localisation
Revenue and sessions are concentrated in a set of large markets such as the United States, India, Canada, the United Kingdom, and several European and Asian countries, making them prime candidates for ongoing optimisation and A/B testing of landing pages, pricing, and promotions. At the same time, smaller markets like Finland, Nigeria, Greece, Vietnam, Kuwait, and Bahrain show very high average LTV and order values, even though absolute traffic is modest.

These high-LTV niches present an opportunity for targeted localisation—such as country-specific promotions, local currency support, and tailored shipping options—that could unlock additional high-value demand with limited incremental spend. For countries with traffic but no or very low purchases, such as Norway and some others, deeper investigation into payment support, shipping costs, or translation coverage may reveal barriers preventing conversion.
### Customer Value and Retention
The presence of customers with multiple orders and elevated lifetime value across several countries indicates that repeat purchasing behaviour exists and can be nurtured. Analysing the acquisition channels, devices, and on-site behaviours of these high-LTV customers can inform targeted retention strategies, loyalty programmes, and personalised recommendations.

Segmenting customers into LTV bands and monitoring how new cohorts migrate across these bands over time would provide a dynamic view of the health of the customer base, beyond single-period revenue. Combining this with funnel and channel metrics enables a holistic view of both short-term performance and long-term customer value creation.
## Recommendations
1. **Prioritise funnel UX improvements at product view and checkout stages.** Focus on increasing the share of sessions that reach product views through better merchandising and navigation, and on reducing checkout friction through UX testing and payment optimisation.
2. **Shift marketing budget towards high-performing organic and referral channels.** Invest in SEO, content, and partnerships that drive high-intent traffic, while critically reviewing paid search campaigns that show relatively low conversion and revenue contribution.
3. **Design targeted localisation and acquisition strategies for high-LTV markets.** For countries with high average LTV but modest volume, test tailored experiences and marketing to grow these profitable segments.
4. **Investigate zero-conversion markets with meaningful traffic.** For markets such as Norway and others with sessions but no recorded purchases, conduct qualitative and technical audits to identify pricing, payment, compliance, or UX barriers.
5. **Institutionalise LTV-based customer segmentation and cohort tracking.** Use the existing `dim_customers` mart and Power BI segments to monitor how different acquisition channels contribute to long-term value, and to guide retention investments.
## Limitations and Next Steps
This analysis is based on one month of data from a sample GA4 dataset, which limits visibility into seasonality, long-term retention, and cohort evolution. Extending the observation window to multiple months and constructing time-based cohorts would enable more robust conclusions about lifetime value dynamics and channel performance over time.

The dataset also reflects the implementation specifics of the GA4 property, including the non-standard relationship between add-to-cart and begin-checkout events, which may not generalise to all e‑commerce sites. Future work could incorporate event-level audits, enhanced event tracking definitions, and cross-validation against order back-end systems to further strengthen data quality and interpretability.
